Say how similar two inputs are. Get a whole distribution over functions.

A nonparametric model needs one piece of prior knowledge: when are two inputs \(\mathbf x\) and \(\mathbf x'\) similar? If they are, the outputs \(f(\mathbf x)\) and \(f(\mathbf x')\) should be similar too. A Mercer kernel \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')\) is a similarity score that follows one strict rule: every Gram matrix it builds is positive definite.

That rule is what lets a kernel act as a covariance. A Gaussian process uses the kernel to say how the function values at any set of points vary together. Observing \(f\) at a few points then tells you about \(f\) everywhere else, through ordinary Gaussian conditioning.

§17.1

Mercer kernels: a similarity score that can't contradict itself

A kernel \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')\) scores how similar two inputs are. To be a Mercer (positive definite) kernel it must be symmetric, \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=K(\mathbf x',\mathbf x)\), and pass a test on every finite set of points: the Gram matrix \(\mathbf K_{ij}=K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\) must be positive definite, meaning \(\sum_i\sum_jK(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)c_ic_j\ge0\) for every choice of numbers \(c_i\) (Eqs. 17.1–17.2).

The workhorse is the squared exponential kernel, also called the RBF, Gaussian or exponentiated quadratic kernel: \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=\exp\!\big(-\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert^2/2\ell^2\big)\) (Eq. 17.3). The length scale \(\ell\), also called the bandwidth, is how far apart two inputs can be before they stop counting as similar.

Explain itAt your level

A kernel is a "how alike are these two?" score.

Give it two things and it gives back a number. Two copies of the same thing get the top score. Things that are very different get a score near zero.

But you can't make up just any scores. Say Ann sits right next to Ben, and Ben sits right next to Cal. A rule like "only people at the same spot count as alike" says Ann and Ben are alike, Ben and Cal are alike, but Ann and Cal are not alike at all. Those scores don't fit together. A Mercer kernel is a scoring rule that never gets tangled up like that.

The most popular rule works like a fading flashlight. The score is brightest when the two things are in the same spot, and it fades smoothly as they move apart. The length scale is how wide the flashlight beam is.

A kernel is a function that takes two inputs and returns a similarity number. The RBF kernel is \(K(x,x')=e^{-(x-x')^2/2\ell^2}\). If \(x=x'\), the score is \(e^0=1\). As the distance grows, the score drops toward 0 along a bell-shaped curve. A bigger \(\ell\) makes the bell wider, so points farther apart still count as similar.

For a list of points \(x_1,\dots,x_N\), put every pair's score in a table. That table is the Gram matrix. It's symmetric, and with the RBF kernel it has 1s down the diagonal.

The Mercer test: choose any weights \(c_1,\dots,c_N\), positive or negative, and add up \(c_ic_jK_{ij}\) over every pair (including each point paired with itself). The total must never come out negative. Later we'll see why: that total is the variance of a weighted sum of function values, and a variance can't be negative.

\(K\) is a Mercer kernel iff for every finite set of distinct inputs, the Gram matrix \(\mathbf K\) is symmetric positive (semi)definite: \(\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K\mathbf c\ge0\) for all \(\mathbf c\in\mathbb R^N\). Since \(\mathbf K\) is symmetric, this is the same as all of its eigenvalues being \(\ge0\): \(\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K\mathbf c=\sum_m\lambda_m(\mathbf u_m^\top\mathbf c)^2\).

Why it matters: in a GP, \(\mathbf K\) is the covariance matrix of \((f(\mathbf x_1),\dots,f(\mathbf x_N))\), and \(\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K\mathbf c=\mathrm{Var}\big[\sum_ic_if(\mathbf x_i)\big]\). A kernel that fails the test would give some combination a negative variance, and the Gaussian would not exist.

Symmetric and "large when close" is not enough. The boxcar similarity \(\mathbb I(|x-x'|\lt\ell)\) looks reasonable but fails with three evenly spaced points. Try it in the Play box.

The RBF kernel's \(\ell\) sets the scale: \(K=e^{-1/2}\approx0.61\) at distance \(\ell\), and \(K\approx0.14\) at \(2\ell\).

  • PD vs PSD. Many authors (and the book) say "positive definite kernel" for what linear algebra calls positive semidefinite Gram matrices. Strict definiteness on distinct points holds for the RBF and Matérn kernels, but not for the linear or polynomial kernels, whose Gram matrices have rank at most the feature dimension.
  • Bochner's theorem. A continuous stationary \(K(\mathbf r)\) on \(\mathbb R^D\) is PSD iff it is the Fourier transform of a nonnegative finite measure. The RBF's spectrum is Gaussian (fine). The boxcar's is a sinc, which goes negative, so some Gram matrices have negative eigenvalues.
  • RKHS. Every PSD kernel defines a unique reproducing kernel Hilbert space \(\mathcal H_K\) with \(\langle f,K(\cdot,\mathbf x)\rangle=f(\mathbf x)\) (Moore–Aronszajn). The kernel methods of Ch. 17 all optimize or integrate over this space.
  • Numerics. RBF Gram matrices are PD in exact arithmetic but become badly conditioned when \(\ell\) is large compared with the point spacing. Their eigenvalues decay super-exponentially. In practice you add jitter \(\epsilon I\) (around \(10^{-6}\)) before the Cholesky factorization.
  • Indefinite "kernels". \(\tanh(a\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x'+b)\) (the sigmoid kernel) is not PSD for most \(a,b\), yet it is used in SVMs anyway. The optimization is then non-convex, and there is no GP interpretation.

Math, slowlyEquations 17.1 – 17.3

The Mercer condition

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\,c_ic_j\ \ge\ 0\qquad\text{for all }N,\ \mathbf x_{1:N},\ \mathbf c\in\mathbb R^N \tag{17.1} \]
\[ \mathbf K=\begin{pmatrix}K(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_1)&\cdots&K(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_N)\\\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\K(\mathbf x_N,\mathbf x_1)&\cdots&K(\mathbf x_N,\mathbf x_N)\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{(17.2)}:\ \ \text{(17.1) says }\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K\mathbf c\ge0 \]
  1. The double sum is just matrix multiplication written out: \(\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K\mathbf c=\sum_ic_i\sum_jK_{ij}c_j\). So "Mercer" means "every Gram matrix is a PSD matrix."
  2. Diagonalize the symmetric matrix: \(\mathbf K=\sum_m\lambda_m\mathbf u_m\mathbf u_m^\top\) with orthonormal \(\mathbf u_m\). Then \[ \mathbf c^\top\mathbf K\mathbf c=\sum_m\lambda_m(\mathbf u_m^\top\mathbf c)^2. \] If all \(\lambda_m\ge0\), every term is \(\ge0\). If some \(\lambda_m\lt0\), choose \(\mathbf c=\mathbf u_m\) and the sum equals \(\lambda_m\lt0\).
  3. So checking (17.1) for one point set means checking the smallest eigenvalue. That's what the Play box does. A kernel is Mercer only if this holds for every point set, which is why one counterexample is enough to rule a kernel out.

Two points, by hand

With \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x)=1\) and \(K(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2)=k\):

\[ \mathbf K=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\k&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda=1\pm k,\qquad \mathbf c^\top\mathbf K\mathbf c=c_1^2+2k\,c_1c_2+c_2^2. \]

It's PSD exactly when \(|k|\le1\): a similarity can't exceed self-similarity. Two points can never break the RBF. You need at least three.

Three points break the box

Box similarity \(K(x,x')=\mathbb I(|x-x'|\lt\ell)\) with \(\ell=1\), at \(x=0,\ 0.8,\ 1.6\). Neighbors are within 1, the ends are 1.6 apart:

\[ \mathbf K=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&1&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda\in\{1+\sqrt2,\ 1,\ 1-\sqrt2\},\qquad 1-\sqrt2\approx-0.414. \]

Take \(\mathbf c=(1,-\sqrt2,1)\): \(\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K\mathbf c=1+2+1-2\sqrt2\cdot2+0=4-4\sqrt2\approx-1.66\lt0\). Points 1 and 3 are each "the same as" point 2 but "nothing like" each other. That's the tangle from the Kid tab.

The RBF kernel

\[ K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert^2}{2\ell^2}\Big) \tag{17.3} \]

At \(\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert=0,\ \ell,\ 2\ell,\ 3\ell\): \(K=1,\ 0.607,\ 0.135,\ 0.011\). Section 17.1.2.2 proves the RBF is a valid kernel (Eq. 17.24).

Fine print: the book assumes \(K\gt0\) and says equality in (17.1) only happens when \(\mathbf c=\mathbf 0\), which is strict definiteness. The linear kernel in the Play box gives a rank-1 Gram matrix, which is PSD but not strictly PD. It's still a valid kernel.

§17.1.1

Mercer's theorem: every kernel is a dot product of hidden features

A PSD matrix can be factored as \(\mathbf K=\mathbf U^\top\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf U\) (rows of \(\mathbf U\) are eigenvectors). Reading off entry \((i,j)\) gives \(k_{ij}=\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_i)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_j)\) with \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_i)=\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}\mathbf U_{:i}\) (Eqs. 17.4–17.5). Every kernel value is an inner product of feature vectors, even if you never write the features down.

Mercer's theorem extends this from matrices to kernel functions. The quadratic kernel \(\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf x'\rangle^2\) in 2-D has the 3-D features \([x_1^2,\sqrt2x_1x_2,x_2^2]\) (Eq. 17.6). The RBF kernel's feature vector is infinitely long, but computing the kernel costs \(O(D)\).

Explain itAt your level

Every thing gets a secret list of numbers.

Imagine each animal gets a card: how furry, how big, how loud. To see how alike two animals are, multiply the matching numbers on their cards and add them up. Big total means very alike.

Mercer's theorem says: every good similarity score works this way, even if you never see the cards. Some cards are short. Some, like the fading-flashlight kernel's, would be infinitely long. The kernel is a shortcut that gives you the total without writing out the card.

The cards can make hard puzzles easy. Dots inside a circle and dots in a ring around it can't be split with a straight line. On their cards (one number for "how far left-right squared", one for "how far up-down squared"), one straight cut separates them.

A dot product multiplies matching entries and adds: \([1,2]\cdot[3,4]=3+8=11\). It's big when two vectors point the same way.

The quadratic kernel \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=(x_1x_1'+x_2x_2')^2\) secretly is a dot product. Expand the square: \(x_1^2x_1'^2+2x_1x_2x_1'x_2'+x_2^2x_2'^2\). That's exactly \([x_1^2,\sqrt2x_1x_2,x_2^2]\cdot[x_1'^2,\sqrt2x_1'x_2',x_2'^2]\). So the kernel moves each 2-D point to a 3-D point and takes the dot product there.

In the new space, the circle \(x_1^2+x_2^2=r^2\) becomes the straight line (flat plane) \(\phi_1+\phi_3=r^2\). A curved boundary became a straight one.

For a Gram matrix: eigendecompose \(\mathbf K=\mathbf V\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf V^\top\) (columns of \(\mathbf V\) are eigenvectors) and define \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_i)=\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}\mathbf V_{i,:}^\top\in\mathbb R^N\). Then \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_i)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_j)=\sum_m\lambda_mV_{im}V_{jm}=K_{ij}\). This needs \(\lambda_m\ge0\) for \(\sqrt{\lambda_m}\) to be real. That is exactly the Mercer condition.

Mercer's theorem (for a continuous PSD kernel on a compact domain): \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_m\psi_m(\mathbf x)\psi_m(\mathbf x')\), where \(\psi_m\) are eigenfunctions of the integral operator \(f\mapsto\int K(\cdot,\mathbf x')f(\mathbf x')\,d\mathbf x'\). So \(\phi_m=\sqrt{\lambda_m}\psi_m\).

The kernel trick: any algorithm that only uses inner products \(\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x'\) can be run in feature space by swapping in \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')\). The cost depends on \(N\), not on the feature dimension.

  • Operator view. \(T_Kf(\mathbf x)=\int K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')f(\mathbf x')\,d\mu(\mathbf x')\) is compact, self-adjoint and positive. Mercer's expansion converges absolutely and uniformly on compact \(\mathcal X\). The Gram matrix eigenvalues \(\lambda_m(\mathbf K)/N\) approximate the operator eigenvalues; this is the basis of the Nyström method.
  • RBF eigensystem. Under a Gaussian input measure, the RBF eigenfunctions are Hermite functions with geometrically decaying eigenvalues, so a handful of features capture most of the kernel. In 1-D, the Taylor series of \(e^{xx'/\ell^2}\) gives explicit features \(\phi_m(x)=e^{-x^2/2\ell^2}x^m/(\ell^m\sqrt{m!})\).
  • Feature maps are not unique. Any \(\boldsymbol\phi'=\mathbf Q\boldsymbol\phi\) with orthogonal \(\mathbf Q\) gives the same kernel. The RKHS is the canonical choice: \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)=K(\cdot,\mathbf x)\).
  • Random Fourier features (Rahimi & Recht 2007). By Bochner, \(K(\mathbf x-\mathbf x')=\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\omega}[\cos(\boldsymbol\omega^\top(\mathbf x-\mathbf x'))]\) with \(\boldsymbol\omega\) drawn from the spectral density. Monte Carlo with \(D\) frequencies gives explicit features with error \(O(D^{-1/2})\). This aside isn't in the book, but it makes "infinite dimensional" concrete. See the last Play panel.

Math, slowlyEquations 17.4 – 17.6

From an eigendecomposition to features

  1. A PSD \(\mathbf K\) factors as \(\mathbf K=\mathbf U^\top\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf U\), with \(\boldsymbol\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_m)\), \(\lambda_m\ge0\). The book's \(\mathbf U\) holds the eigenvectors as rows, so column \(\mathbf U_{:i}\) lists every eigenvector's \(i\)-th entry.
  2. Entry \((i,j)\): \(k_{ij}=\sum_m U_{mi}\lambda_mU_{mj}=\sum_m(\sqrt{\lambda_m}U_{mi})(\sqrt{\lambda_m}U_{mj})\). \[ k_{ij}=(\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}\mathbf U_{:i})^\top(\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}\mathbf U_{:j}) \tag{17.4} \]
  3. Name the vectors: \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_i)=\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}\mathbf U_{:i}\). \[ k_{ij}=\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_i)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_j)=\sum_m\phi_m(\mathbf x_i)\phi_m(\mathbf x_j) \tag{17.5} \] \(\sqrt{\lambda_m}\) is real only if \(\lambda_m\ge0\). PSD and "is a Gram matrix of real vectors" are the same thing.

The quadratic kernel in 2-D

\[ K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf x'\rangle^2=(x_1x_1'+x_2x_2')^2=x_1^2x_1'^2+2x_1x_2x_1'x_2'+x_2^2x_2'^2 \tag{17.6} \]

Split \(2=\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2\) so each term is a product of something about \(\mathbf x\) and the same thing about \(\mathbf x'\):

\[ \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)=\big[x_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,x_1x_2,\ x_2^2\big]\in\mathbb R^3,\qquad K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x'). \]

Worked example. \(\mathbf x=(1,2)\), \(\mathbf x'=(3,-1)\). Kernel: \((3-2)^2=1\). Features: \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)=[1,2\sqrt2,4]\), \(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x')=[9,-3\sqrt2,1]\), and \(9-12+4=1\). Same answer, but the kernel took 3 multiplications instead of building two 3-vectors.

The RBF kernel's features are infinite (1-D sketch)

\[ e^{-(x-x')^2/2\ell^2}=e^{-x^2/2\ell^2}\,e^{-x'^2/2\ell^2}\,e^{xx'/\ell^2}=e^{-x^2/2\ell^2}e^{-x'^2/2\ell^2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(xx')^m}{\ell^{2m}m!} \]

So \(\phi_m(x)=e^{-x^2/2\ell^2}\,\dfrac{x^m}{\ell^m\sqrt{m!}}\) for \(m=0,1,2,\dots\) One feature for every power of \(x\), with no end.

Why bother with features at all? They prove the kernel is valid, because a Gram matrix of real feature vectors is always PSD: \(\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K\mathbf c=\lVert\sum_ic_i\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_i)\rVert^2\ge0\). And they explain what the model can represent.

§17.1.2.1

Stationary kernels: only the distance matters

A stationary kernel depends only on the difference \(\mathbf r=\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\), so the same rule of similarity applies everywhere in input space. Each one encodes a different belief about what \(f\) looks like. The best way to see that belief is to sample functions from a GP that uses the kernel as its covariance (previewing §17.2).

The ARD kernel gives each input dimension its own length scale (Eqs. 17.7–17.9). Matérn kernels make rougher functions (Eqs. 17.10–17.13). Periodic and cosine kernels make repeating ones (Eqs. 17.14–17.15).

Explain itAt your level

Each kernel is a different robot that draws squiggles.

One robot (the RBF) has a super steady hand. Its lines are always smooth and gently curving. Another (Matérn ½) has a shaky hand, so its lines are jagged like a mountain range. The Matérn 3/2 and 5/2 robots are somewhere in between.

The length scale is how far the robot's pencil travels before it changes its mind about going up or down. Short means lots of wiggles. Long means slow, lazy hills.

The periodic robot draws a pattern and then repeats it forever, like waves at the beach.

"Stationary" means the kernel only cares about how far apart two inputs are, \(r=|x-x'|\), not where they are. Plot \(K\) against \(r\): it starts at its maximum when \(r=0\) and falls as \(r\) grows (except periodic kernels, which come back up every period \(p\)).

The shape of that fall-off controls how smooth the functions are. The RBF is rounded at the top, so its functions are smooth. Matérn ½ is \(e^{-r/\ell}\), which has a sharp corner at \(r=0\), so its functions are jagged.

With 2-D inputs, the ARD kernel gives each direction its own \(\ell\). A huge \(\ell_2\) means "changing \(x_2\) barely changes anything", so the function looks like stripes running along \(x_2\): that input is ignored.

  • ARD (17.7–17.9): \(K(\mathbf r)=\sigma^2\exp(-\tfrac12\mathbf r^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf r)\). With diagonal \(\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\ell_d^2)\) it factors into a product of 1-D kernels. Setting \(\ell_d\to\infty\) removes dimension \(d\). \(\sigma^2=K(\mathbf 0)\) is the prior variance of \(f(\mathbf x)\).
  • Matérn (17.10): sample paths are \(k\)-times differentiable iff \(\nu\gt k\). Half-integer \(\nu\) gives closed forms (17.11–17.13). \(\nu=\tfrac12\) is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: continuous, nowhere differentiable. As \(\nu\to\infty\) it becomes the SE kernel.
  • Periodic (17.14): \(\exp(-\tfrac2{\ell^2}\sin^2(\pi r/p))\). It equals 1 whenever \(r\) is a multiple of \(p\), so \(f(x)\) and \(f(x+p)\) are perfectly correlated. \(\ell\) controls how wiggly each period is.
  • Cosine (17.15): \(\cos(2\pi r/p)\) gives pure sinusoids of period \(p\) with random amplitude and phase. Its Gram matrices have rank 2.
  • Spectral view. By Bochner, a stationary kernel is set by its spectral density \(S(s)\). SE: Gaussian \(S\), so high frequencies are suppressed super-exponentially and paths are \(C^\infty\). Matérn: \(S(s)\propto(2\nu/\ell^2+4\pi^2s^2)^{-(\nu+D/2)}\), a polynomial tail. Smoothness is the tail exponent.
  • SE is often too smooth. Stein (1999) argues for Matérn: SE posteriors become overconfident when interpolating rough real-world data. Matérn 5/2 is the default in most Bayesian optimization libraries.
  • ARD caveat. A small \(\ell_d\) means \(f\) varies quickly along \(d\), not that \(d\) is important. A strong but linear effect can get a long length scale. Length scales measure nonlinearity, and relevance only roughly.
  • Periodic kernel derivation (MacKay): warp \(x\mapsto\mathbf u=(\cos\frac{2\pi x}p,\sin\frac{2\pi x}p)\) and apply an SE kernel to \(\mathbf u\). Then \(\lVert\mathbf u-\mathbf u'\rVert^2=4\sin^2(\pi r/p)\), which gives Eq. 17.14 up to the convention for \(\ell\).
  • State-space trick. Matérn kernels with half-integer \(\nu\) are 1-D linear SDEs, so GP regression can run in \(O(N)\) with a Kalman filter instead of \(O(N^3)\).

ARD: one length scale per dimension

\[ K(\mathbf r)=\sigma^2\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\mathbf r^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf r\Big),\qquad\mathbf r=\mathbf x-\mathbf x' \tag{17.7} \]
  1. Take \(\boldsymbol\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\ell_1^2,\dots,\ell_D^2)\). Then \(\mathbf r^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf r=\sum_dr_d^2/\ell_d^2\). This is a Mahalanobis distance that rescales each axis.
  2. The exponential of a sum is a product of exponentials. Splitting \(\sigma^2=\prod_d(\sigma^2)^{1/D}\): \[ K(\mathbf r;\boldsymbol\ell,\sigma^2)=\sigma^2\exp\!\Big(-\frac12\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{r_d^2}{\ell_d^2}\Big)=\prod_{d=1}^{D}K(r_d;\ell_d,\sigma^{2/D}) \tag{17.8} \] \[ K(r;\ell,\tau^2)=\tau^2\exp\!\Big(-\frac12\frac{r^2}{\ell^2}\Big) \tag{17.9} \] The book writes \(\sigma^2/D\) for the per-factor variance. For the product to equal \(\sigma^2\) the factors have to be \(\sigma^{2/D}\), since a product of \(D\) factors each \(\sigma^2/D\) would be \((\sigma^2/D)^D\).
  3. As \(\ell_d\to\infty\), \(r_d^2/\ell_d^2\to0\) and that factor becomes 1. Dimension \(d\) drops out entirely. That's "automatic relevancy determination."

The ARD heat map above uses this product structure: sample a 60 × 60 grid as \(\mathbf F=\mathbf L_1\mathbf Z\mathbf L_2^\top\), with \(\mathbf L_d\) the Cholesky factor of the 1-D Gram matrix for axis \(d\).

Matérn kernels

\[ K(r;\nu,\ell)=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,r}{\ell}\Big)^{\nu}K_\nu\Big(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,r}{\ell}\Big) \tag{17.10} \]

\(K_\nu\) here is a modified Bessel function, not a kernel. For half-integers it collapses to a polynomial times an exponential. Write \(a=\sqrt{2\nu}\,r/\ell\):

\[ \nu=\tfrac12:\ e^{-r/\ell}\quad(17.11)\qquad \nu=\tfrac32:\ (1+a)e^{-a}\quad(17.12)\qquad \nu=\tfrac52:\ \Big(1+a+\frac{a^2}{3}\Big)e^{-a}\quad(17.13) \]

(With \(a=\sqrt5r/\ell\), \(a^2/3=5r^2/3\ell^2\), as in Eq. 17.13.) Smoothness test: look at \(K\) near \(r=0\). Matérn ½ has slope \(-1/\ell\) at 0, a corner. That corner gives non-differentiable paths. Matérn 3/2 is flat at 0 (\(\frac{d}{da}(1+a)e^{-a}=-ae^{-a}=0\) at \(a=0\)), which gives once-differentiable paths.

Periodic and cosine kernels

\[ K_{\text{per}}(r;\ell,p)=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2}{\ell^2}\sin^2\!\Big(\pi\frac rp\Big)\Big) \tag{17.14} \]
\[ K(r;p)=\cos\!\Big(2\pi\frac rp\Big) \tag{17.15} \]

\(\sin^2(\pi r/p)=0\) at \(r=0,p,2p,\dots\), so \(K_{\text{per}}=1\) there: values one period apart are perfectly correlated. Its minimum, at \(r=p/2\), is \(e^{-2/\ell^2}\).

The cosine kernel is a feature map in disguise: \(\cos(a-b)=\cos a\cos b+\sin a\sin b\) with \(a=2\pi x/p\), \(b=2\pi x'/p\). So \(\boldsymbol\phi(x)=[\cos\frac{2\pi x}p,\sin\frac{2\pi x}p]\), two features, and every sample is \(w_1\cos+w_2\sin\): a pure sinusoid.

§17.1.2.2–4

New kernels from old: add for "or", multiply for "and"

You rarely need to prove from scratch that a kernel is valid. Start from valid kernels and apply rules that preserve validity: scale by \(c\gt0\), sandwich between \(f(\mathbf x)\) and \(f(\mathbf x')\), apply a polynomial with nonnegative coefficients or \(\exp\), or use \(\mathbf x^\top\mathbf A\mathbf x'\) with PSD \(\mathbf A\) (Eqs. 17.16–17.20). These rules give the polynomial kernels and a short proof that the Gaussian kernel is valid (17.21–17.24).

Adding two kernels (17.25) gives functions with either kind of structure, a disjunction. Multiplying (17.26) gives functions with both, a conjunction. Kernels also work on structured inputs like strings and graphs, where there's no obvious vector to compute distances from (§17.1.2.4).

Explain itAt your level

Kernels snap together like Lego.

Adding two kernels is like a song with a drum beat plus a slow melody. You hear both patterns stacked on top of each other: a wave riding on a hill.

Multiplying is like a rule that says "these two must be alike in both ways." A wave times a "nearby" rule gives a wave that slowly changes shape as you go along, because spots far apart aren't alike any more, even if they're at the same point in the wave.

Words can have kernels too. "kernel" and "kennel" share lots of little letter chunks ("ke", "el", "ne"), so they score as alike.

If \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) are valid, so are \(K_1+K_2\), \(K_1\times K_2\), \(3K_1\), \(K_1^2\), \(e^{K_1}\), and more. So you can build a kernel to match what you believe about the data.

Sum: a function from \(K_1+K_2\) looks like a function from \(K_1\) plus an independent function from \(K_2\). Linear + periodic = a repeating pattern on a straight-line trend.

Product: \(K_1K_2\) is large only when both are large. SE × periodic: points must be one period apart and close by to be strongly linked, so the repeating pattern drifts slowly.

Linear × linear \(=(xx')^2\) gives parabolas. Raising \(\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x'\) to the power \(M\) gives all the degree-\(M\) terms, so polynomial regression can be done with a kernel.

  • Sum: \(\mathbf c^\top(\mathbf K_1+\mathbf K_2)\mathbf c=\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K_1\mathbf c+\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K_2\mathbf c\ge0\). In GP terms, \(f=f_1+f_2\) with independent \(f_i\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,K_i)\).
  • Product: the Gram matrix is the elementwise (Hadamard) product \(\mathbf K_1\circ\mathbf K_2\), which is PSD by the Schur product theorem. In feature terms, \(\boldsymbol\phi=\boldsymbol\phi_1\otimes\boldsymbol\phi_2\): every product of a feature from each.
  • 17.17: \(\mathbf K'=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf f)\,\mathbf K\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf f)\), so \(\mathbf c^\top\mathbf K'\mathbf c=(\mathbf f\circ\mathbf c)^\top\mathbf K(\mathbf f\circ\mathbf c)\ge0\). 17.18–17.19 follow from sums, products and limits (\(e^K\) is a power series with positive coefficients).
  • Polynomial kernels: \((\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x')^M\) has every degree-\(M\) monomial as a feature. \((\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x'+c)^M\) has every monomial of degree \(\le M\), \(\binom{D+M}{M}\) of them, at \(O(D)\) cost.
  • String kernel: \(\phi_s(\text{str})=\) number of times n-gram \(s\) occurs. \(K\) is a dot product of count vectors, so it's valid automatically.
  • Kernel grammars. Duvenaud et al.'s compositional search (the "Automatic Statistician") searches over sums and products of {SE, Lin, Per, RQ} by marginal likelihood, then reads the result as English: "a linearly growing periodic component plus a smooth trend."
  • Additive GPs. \(\sum_dK_d(x_d,x_d')\) restricts \(f\) to a sum of 1-D functions. That avoids the curse of dimensionality but can't model interactions. ANOVA kernels interpolate between additive and full product.
  • Normalization. \(\tilde K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=K/\sqrt{K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x)K(\mathbf x',\mathbf x')}\) is Eq. 17.17 with \(f=K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x)^{-1/2}\). It is the cosine of the angle between feature vectors, and it matters for string kernels, where long strings would otherwise dominate.
  • Structured kernels in practice. Gappy subsequence kernels via dynamic programming [Lod+02]; Weisfeiler–Lehman subtree kernels for graphs (near linear time, a strong baseline that GNNs match in expressive power); random walk kernels via Kronecker-product linear systems [Vis+10].
  • Not closed under everything. \(K_1-K_2\), \(\min(K_1,K_2)\) and \(\sqrt{K_1}\) can all fail. The Play box's eigenvalue check is a quick sanity test for a given point set, not a proof.

§17.2

Gaussian processes: every finite set of function values is jointly Gaussian

A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions \(f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R\), written \(f\sim\mathrm{GP}(m,K)\). The definition: for any finite set of inputs, the vector \([f(\mathbf x_1),\dots,f(\mathbf x_M)]\) is Gaussian with mean \(m(\mathbf x_i)\) and covariance \(\Sigma_{ij}=K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\). This is why \(K\) has to be Mercer: \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) must be a valid covariance matrix.

Take \(N\) training inputs plus one test input \(\mathbf x_*\). The \(N+1\) function values are jointly Gaussian, so learning \(f(\mathbf x_1),\dots,f(\mathbf x_N)\) tells you about \(f(\mathbf x_*)\) through Gaussian conditioning (Figure 17.6). The more correlated \(\mathbf x_*\) is with the training inputs, the more it learns.

Explain itAt your level

A machine that dreams up wiggly lines.

Before it sees any data, a Gaussian process can draw lots of pretend lines. It follows one rule: spots that are close together go up and down together.

So if you peek at one spot and see that the line is high there, you know the line is probably high just next to it too. Far away, peeking doesn't help much: the line there could be anywhere.

The kernel is the rule that says how close counts as "close together."

Pick any two inputs \(x_1\) and \(x_*\). Under a GP, the pair \((f(x_1),f(x_*))\) is a 2-D bell curve: a cloud of points shaped like an ellipse. The kernel value \(K(x_1,x_*)\) is the correlation.

If \(x_1\) and \(x_*\) are close, the correlation is near 1. The cloud is a thin diagonal ellipse, and knowing \(f(x_1)\) pins down \(f(x_*)\). If they're far apart, the correlation is near 0. The cloud is round, and knowing \(f(x_1)\) tells you almost nothing.

Slice the ellipse at the observed value of \(f(x_1)\): the slice is a narrower bell curve for \(f(x_*)\). That's the prediction.

Definition: \(f\sim\mathrm{GP}(m,K)\) iff for every \(M\) and \(\mathbf x_{1:M}\), \(\mathbf f=[f(\mathbf x_1),\dots,f(\mathbf x_M)]\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)\) with \(\mu_i=m(\mathbf x_i)\), \(\Sigma_{ij}=K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)\).

Two-point case (zero mean, \(K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x)=1\), \(\rho=K(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_*)\)): by the Gaussian conditioning formula, \(f_*\mid f_1\sim\mathcal N(\rho f_1,\ 1-\rho^2)\). The prediction shrinks \(f_1\) toward the prior mean 0 by the factor \(\rho\), and the variance drops by \(\rho^2\).

Figure 17.6 draws this as a graphical model: the latent \(f_i\) are fully connected by undirected edges (a Gaussian graphical model), and each observed \(y_i\) hangs off its own \(f_i\).

  • Existence. The finite-dimensional Gaussians are consistent under marginalization (drop a row/column of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\)) and permutation, so Kolmogorov's extension theorem gives a process on \(\mathbb R^{\mathcal X}\). Path properties like continuity need more (e.g. Kolmogorov–Chentsov, or the spectral conditions behind Matérn smoothness).
  • Weight-space view. \(f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)\) with \(\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)\) is a GP with \(K=\boldsymbol\phi^\top\boldsymbol\phi'\). Mercer says every GP is (a limit of) this, with possibly infinitely many features. That's how GPs connect to Bayesian linear regression and to infinitely wide neural networks.
  • Edges in Fig. 17.6. The covariance \(\Sigma_{ij}\) is dense. The graphical-model edges really encode the precision \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\), which for a GP is also generally dense. Sparse precisions come from Markov kernels like Matérn ½ in 1-D, which gives a tridiagonal precision (a chain).
  • Mean function. Usually \(m\equiv0\) after centering \(y\). A parametric mean \(m(\mathbf x)=\boldsymbol\beta^\top\mathbf h(\mathbf x)\) with a Gaussian prior on \(\boldsymbol\beta\) folds into the kernel as \(K+\mathbf h^\top\mathbf B\mathbf h'\).

§17.2.1

Noise-free observations: an interpolator with honest error bars

Observe \(y_n=f(\mathbf x_n)\) exactly. The joint of training values \(\mathbf f_X\) and test values \(\mathbf f_*\) is Gaussian (Eq. 17.27), so the posterior at the test points is Gaussian with mean and covariance given by Eqs. 17.28–17.30.

The posterior mean passes through every training point, and the variance there is exactly 0: the GP interpolates. Between points the uncertainty grows, and far from all data it returns to the prior (Figure 17.7).

Explain itAt your level

Connect the dots with a fuzzy marker.

The machine draws a line that goes exactly through every dot you give it, because those dots are true. Right at a dot the line is sharp. Between dots, it gets fuzzy, because it's not sure. Far away from all the dots, it's very fuzzy and drifts back to its starting guess.

Add a dot in a fuzzy area, and the fuzz there shrinks.

With \(\boldsymbol\mu=0\): \(\boldsymbol\mu_*=\mathbf K_{X,*}^\top\mathbf K_{X,X}^{-1}\mathbf f_X\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_*=\mathbf K_{*,*}-\mathbf K_{X,*}^\top\mathbf K_{X,X}^{-1}\mathbf K_{X,*}\).

Interpolation proof: if \(\mathbf x_*=\mathbf x_n\), then \(\mathbf k_*\) is column \(n\) of \(\mathbf K\), so \(\mathbf K^{-1}\mathbf k_*=\mathbf e_n\). Mean: \(\mathbf e_n^\top\mathbf f_X=f_n\). Variance: \(K_{nn}-\mathbf k_*^\top\mathbf e_n=K_{nn}-K_{nn}=0\).

Note that \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_*\) doesn't depend on \(\mathbf f_X\) at all. The error bars depend only on where you observed, not on what you saw.

The table in the Play box breaks \(\mu_*=\sum_n k_{*n}\alpha_n\) into its terms, with \(\boldsymbol\alpha=\mathbf K^{-1}\mathbf f_X\).

  • Kriging / RKHS view. The posterior mean is the minimum-RKHS-norm interpolant: \(\arg\min\{\lVert g\rVert_{\mathcal H_K}: g(\mathbf x_n)=f_n\}\). The posterior std is the worst-case error over the unit ball of \(\mathcal H_K\) (the "power function").
  • Conditioning. Two close inputs make \(\mathbf K\) nearly singular, and \(\mathbf K^{-1}\mathbf f\) explodes when their \(f\) values disagree. Add two nearby points with different heights and watch \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) and cond(\(\mathbf K\)). Real code adds jitter \(\epsilon\mathbf I\), which is a tiny noise term (§17.2.2).
  • Cost. One Cholesky \(\mathbf K=\mathbf L\mathbf L^\top\) at \(O(N^3)\). Then each test mean is \(O(N)\) (dot with \(\boldsymbol\alpha\)) and each variance is \(O(N^2)\) (a triangular solve).
  • Where it's used. Deterministic computer simulators (emulators), Bayesian optimization of noise-free objectives, and design of experiments, where the variance-only-depends-on-X property lets you plan where to sample before seeing any results.

§17.2.2

Noisy observations: add σy² to the diagonal

Now observe \(y_n=f(\mathbf x_n)+\epsilon_n\) with \(\epsilon_n\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_y^2)\). Noise is independent across points, so it only adds to the diagonal of the covariance: \(\mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf y\mid X]=\mathbf K_{X,X}+\sigma_y^2\mathbf I_N\triangleq\mathbf K_\sigma\) (Eqs. 17.31–17.32). Everything else is the noise-free formula with \(\mathbf K_\sigma\) in place of \(\mathbf K_{X,X}\) (17.33–17.37).

The posterior mean no longer has to pass through the data. It is a weighted sum of kernel bumps, one per training point: \(\mu_*=\sum_nK(\mathbf x_*,\mathbf x_n)\alpha_n\) with \(\boldsymbol\alpha=\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}\mathbf y\) (17.38). This is the same predictor as kernel ridge regression.

Explain itAt your level

Now the dots are a bit wobbly.

Imagine measuring with a shaky ruler. Each dot is near the true line but not exactly on it. If the machine went through every dot, it would make a crazy zig-zag line.

So instead it draws a smooth line that goes close to the dots, like drawing through the middle of a cloud. The shakier the ruler, the less it trusts any single dot.

The answer is built from little hills, one for each dot. Some hills are tall, some are small, some point down. Add them up and you get the line.

Each measurement \(y_n\) is the true value plus random noise with standard deviation \(\sigma_y\). The GP knows this, so it doesn't force the curve through the points.

Turn \(\sigma_y\) down to almost 0 and you get the zig-zag interpolator from §17.2.1. Turn it up and the curve flattens toward 0, because it believes most of what it sees is noise. In between is a smooth fit.

The band no longer pinches to zero at the data. Even right at a measurement, you don't know \(f\) exactly. The dashed outer band is for a new noisy measurement \(y_*\), so it's wider by the noise.

Eq. 17.37 for one test point: \(\mu_*=\mathbf k_*^\top\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}\mathbf y\), \(\sigma_*^2=k_{**}-\mathbf k_*^\top\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}\mathbf k_*\). For a noisy \(y_*\), add \(\sigma_y^2\).

Eq. 17.38: \(\mu_*=\sum_n\alpha_nK(\mathbf x_*,\mathbf x_n)\) is a linear combination of \(N\) kernel functions centered on the training inputs. Turn on "show each term" to see the bumps \(\alpha_nK(x,x_n)\) add up to the mean.

Same formula as kernel ridge regression with \(\lambda=\sigma_y^2\): \(\min_f\sum_n(y_n-f(\mathbf x_n))^2+\sigma_y^2\lVert f\rVert^2_{\mathcal H}\). The GP adds the variance.

Compare with Nadaraya-Watson (§16.3.5): N-W's weights \(w_n(\mathbf x_*)\) are positive and sum to 1. The GP's weights \(\mathbf k_*^\top\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}\) can be negative.

  • Nugget and conditioning. \(\sigma_y^2\mathbf I\) bounds the smallest eigenvalue of \(\mathbf K_\sigma\) below by \(\sigma_y^2\), so cond(\(\mathbf K_\sigma\)) \(\le1+\lambda_{\max}/\sigma_y^2\). Jitter is just a tiny nugget.
  • Smoother matrix. The fitted values are \(\hat{\mathbf f}=\mathbf K(\mathbf K+\sigma_y^2\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf y=\mathbf S\mathbf y\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_m/(\lambda_m+\sigma_y^2)\). Effective degrees of freedom: \(\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf S=\sum_m\lambda_m/(\lambda_m+\sigma_y^2)\). Directions with \(\lambda_m\ll\sigma_y^2\) are shrunk away.
  • Equivalent kernel. \(\mathbf h(\mathbf x_*)=\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}\mathbf k_*\) gives \(\mu_*=\mathbf h^\top\mathbf y\). For dense data it approaches a fixed local smoother (Silverman 1984), which looks like a kernel with negative side lobes.
  • Hyperparameters. \(\ell\), \(\sigma_f\) and \(\sigma_y\) are usually fit by maximizing the marginal likelihood \(\log\mathcal N(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf 0,\mathbf K_\sigma)\) (§17.2.6). Try the data-noise vs model-σy sliders to see what mismatch looks like.
  • Representer theorem. The 17.38 form, a finite sum of \(K(\cdot,\mathbf x_n)\), is the general shape of the optimum of any loss + RKHS-norm penalty. That's why SVMs (§17.3) also end up as weighted sums of kernels.

Math, slowlyEquations 17.31 – 17.38

Noise only touches the diagonal

\[ \mathrm{Cov}[y_i,y_j]=\mathrm{Cov}[f_i,f_j]+\mathrm{Cov}[\epsilon_i,\epsilon_j]=K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)+\sigma_y^2\delta_{ij} \tag{17.31} \]
  1. \(\mathrm{Cov}[f_i+\epsilon_i,f_j+\epsilon_j]\) expands into four terms. The cross terms \(\mathrm{Cov}[f_i,\epsilon_j]\) vanish because the noise is independent of \(f\).
  2. \(\mathrm{Cov}[\epsilon_i,\epsilon_j]=\sigma_y^2\) if \(i=j\), else 0. Independent noise across points: \(\delta_{ij}=\mathbb I(i=j)\). \[ \mathrm{Cov}[\mathbf y\mid X]=\mathbf K_{X,X}+\sigma_y^2\mathbf I_N\triangleq\mathbf K_\sigma \tag{17.32} \]
  3. The test values are the noise-free \(\mathbf f_*\), and their covariance with \(\mathbf y\) is still \(\mathbf K_{X,*}\) (noise is independent of everything): \[ \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf y\\\mathbf f_*\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal N\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\mu_X\\\boldsymbol\mu_*\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf K_\sigma&\mathbf K_{X,*}\\\mathbf K_{X,*}^\top&\mathbf K_{*,*}\end{pmatrix}\right) \tag{17.33} \]

The posterior predictive

\[ p(\mathbf f_*\mid\mathcal D,X_*)=\mathcal N(\mathbf f_*\mid\boldsymbol\mu_{*|X},\boldsymbol\Sigma_{*|X}) \tag{17.34} \]
\[ \boldsymbol\mu_{*|X}=\boldsymbol\mu_*+\mathbf K_{X,*}^\top\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu_X) \tag{17.35} \]
\[ \boldsymbol\Sigma_{*|X}=\mathbf K_{*,*}-\mathbf K_{X,*}^\top\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}\mathbf K_{X,*} \tag{17.36} \]

One test input, with \(\mathbf k_*=[K(\mathbf x_*,\mathbf x_1),\dots,K(\mathbf x_*,\mathbf x_N)]\) and \(k_{**}=K(\mathbf x_*,\mathbf x_*)\):

\[ p(f_*\mid\mathcal D,\mathbf x_*)=\mathcal N\big(f_*\mid m_*+\mathbf k_*^\top\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu_X),\ k_{**}-\mathbf k_*^\top\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}\mathbf k_*\big) \tag{17.37} \]
\[ \mu_{*|X}=\mathbf k_*^\top(\mathbf K_\sigma^{-1}\mathbf y)\triangleq\mathbf k_*^\top\boldsymbol\alpha=\sum_{n=1}^{N}K(\mathbf x_*,\mathbf x_n)\,\alpha_n\qquad(m\equiv0) \tag{17.38} \]

Worked example, N = 1

SE, \(\ell=1\), one observation \(y=1\) at \(x=0\), \(\sigma_y^2=0.25\). Then \(K_\sigma=1.25\) and \(\alpha=1/1.25=0.8\).

  • At \(x_*=0\): \(\mu_*=1\times0.8=0.8\), not 1: the GP shrinks toward the prior mean because the point might be noise. \(\sigma_*^2=1-1/1.25=0.2\), so \(\sigma_*=0.447\), not 0.
  • For a new measurement at \(x_*=0\): \(\mathrm{Var}[y_*]=0.2+0.25=0.45\).
  • Shrinkage factor \(K/(K+\sigma_y^2)=1/(1+0.25)\): a signal-to-noise ratio.
Kernel ridge regression (§17.3.9, Eq. 17.108) minimizes \(\sum_n(y_n-f(\mathbf x_n))^2+\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal H}^2\) and gets \(f(\mathbf x_*)=\mathbf k_*^\top(\mathbf K+\lambda\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf y\). Set \(\lambda=\sigma_y^2\) and it's Eq. 17.38. The GP's extra contribution is the error bar.
